Grief that got no funeral

No one gave you permission for this pain. There was no service, no time off, and no one asked how you were doing. Some of the heaviest losses are the ones nobody recognizes.

The loss no one holds a service for

Grief isn't only death. It's the fifteen-year friendship that ended without a fight, the relationship that took the house and the mutual friends with it, the person who moved across the world, the version of you that got left behind. In 1989 a psychologist gave it a name: grief with no social licence to exist.

Because no one recognizes it, you're expected to be fine, and the pressure to be fine becomes a second grief stacked on the first.

Presence instead of rushing past

The tradition around Kshitigarbha doesn't promise the pain will stop, because no one honestly can. What it offers is grounded presence: a way to stay with a loss without freezing in it, and without accepting the world's demand that you hurry through. The research the book opens with overturned a comfortable idea: that moving on requires letting go. It usually doesn't.

A practice for unrecognized grief

Whenever the loss returns and there's no one to tell.

  1. Name it as grief. Give the loss its real name, even if no one else would call it that. Recognition is the thing the world withheld.
  2. Drop the deadline. There is no schedule you're behind on. Let the feeling take the time it takes.
  3. Keep the connection, not just the absence. Moving on doesn't require severing the bond. Let what mattered stay part of you.
  4. Do one small kind thing. For grief with nowhere to go, a single act of care, for yourself or someone else, is enough for today.
