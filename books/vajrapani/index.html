About the book

Most of us learned two ways to handle anger: swallow it or let it rip. Both come with a bill.

Swallow it, and it comes back weeks later as resentment, usually aimed at the wrong person. Let it rip, and you spend the following days cleaning up. Neither one solves anything, and both teach the same bad lesson: that anger is a flaw, and the goal is to have less of it.

Vajrapani's name means “vajra in hand”, and in Tibetan art he looks nothing like calm: dark blue, ringed by flames, fangs showing, planted in a fighter's stance. Tradition places him beside Avalokiteshvara and Manjushri as one of the three great protectors, standing for compassion, wisdom and power. All that ferocity points inward, at whatever takes your freedom without asking: the fear that shuts you up, the pride that swings first, the laziness that passes for prudence, the confusion that swears nothing can be done.

Vajrapani reads that image as instructions. Feel the anger all the way, then give it a direction. Much of the book deals with boundaries: how to draw one without attacking anyone, how to hold it when it gets tested, how to defend what's true for you and still leave the other person standing.

Anger shows up on almost every page, because it's the raw material most people waste. The real subject is power: how to take it back from fear, aim it well, and keep it going over time without turning into the person you can't stand.

Who it's for

  • People who swallow the insult on the spot and find the resentment weeks later
  • Anyone who blows up and then spends days on damage control
  • People who can say no inside their heads but never out loud
  • Anyone stuck in a situation that won't budge and feeling powerless about it
  • Readers who want to read anger as information instead of just venting it

What's inside

  • What to do in the half-second between trigger and reaction

Frequently asked

So should I express my anger or not?

Neither bottle it up nor dump it on someone. The book treats anger as energy with a message in it. The job is to read where it points and put its strength to work.

I'm terrible at setting boundaries. Will this help?

That is the heart of the book: how to phrase a boundary, say it out loud and hold it without aggression.

How does it connect to Tara?

Closely. Fear freezes and anger pushes, and very often it's one energy moving in two directions, so the two books complement each other. Each one still stands on its own.
