About the book

Your mind convenes a committee at 2 a.m. This book is the adjournment procedure.

You replay a conversation from three years ago. You draft the message you will never send, revise it forty times, and deliver it to nobody — in the car. You research a decision for five weeks and somehow know less than when you started. And everyone keeps telling you to “let it go”, as if you hadn't thought of that. As if thinking were the problem.

Here is the part nobody told you: the Buddha left a protocol for this. Not a vibe. Not a poster. An actual, sequential, five-step procedure for what to do when a thought will not leave — plus twenty other practices for the loop, the worry, the grudge, the guilt, and the decision you keep not making.

Every one of the 21 practices comes with a named source: a specific discourse or classical manual you can read tonight, free, with the book showing you exactly where. And every one comes with exact instructions — what to do, how long to do it, and what it feels like when it starts working. No “sit with it”. No “observe without judgment” and good luck.

This is not a book about emptying your mind, escaping your life, or becoming permanently calm. It is about recognizing the loop, interrupting it, and getting back to what you were doing. Practical Buddhism for 2 a.m., for traffic, for the difficult conversation, and for every case your mind insists on reopening.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to meditate to use the practices?

No. Several practices work standing up, in traffic, or in the middle of a meeting. Formal meditation is an option, not a prerequisite.

Is this book part of The Technologies of the Awakened Mind?

No. It is a standalone work, more direct and funnier, though it shares the same approach: method before doctrine.

Can I read it out of order?

Yes, and that is how it was written. Each of the 21 practices is self-contained, has its own named source and its own instructions; go straight to the one that matches your kind of loop.

Does it replace therapy?

No. The book offers contemplative practices for everyday life and is not a substitute for mental health treatment.

About the author

Hugo Salles is a writer, lawyer and executive. For over twenty years he has led teams and projects — an experience that brought his study of Mahayana and Tibetan Buddhism into contact with the concrete problems of everyday life. He has translated selected Buddhist sutras into Portuguese and writes The Technologies of the Awakened Mind.
