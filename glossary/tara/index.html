Tara (Noble Tara)

Tara (Sanskrit: तारा, Tārā; Tibetan: སྒྲོལ་མ, Drolma) is the figure the Tibetan tradition turns to when the problem is fear. Her Sanskrit name can mean both “star” and “she who carries across”; the Tibetan name means “she who frees”. Tibetans regard her as a fully awakened Buddha who acts with a bodhisattva's urgency.

Born from a tear

A Tibetan account places her origin in Avalokiteshvara's compassion. Looking out at the suffering of the world, he wept, and from one of his tears a lotus opened with Tara seated on it. The image says a lot in little space: Tara is what compassion looks like once it stops weeping and gets up.

The princess who kept her body

Tāranātha's history of the Tara tradition, The Golden Rosary (1604), tells of a princess named Jñānacandrā who, close to awakening, was advised by monks to pray for rebirth as a man. She refused. In the nature of mind, she answered, there is no male and no female, and she vowed to work for beings in a woman's body until samsara is empty.

The posture and the eight fears

Green Tara, the most widespread form, sits with the left leg folded in meditation and the right leg extended, the foot resting on a small lotus, ready to step down. Classical praises list eight great dangers she protects from: lions, elephants, fire, snakes, thieves, chains, floods and flesh-eating spirits. Tibetan commentaries, such as the First Dalai Lama's praise of Tara who protects from the eight dangers, read them as inner states: pride, delusion, anger, jealousy, wrong views, avarice, attachment and doubt.

The master Atiśa, who arrived in Tibet in 1042, relied on Tara throughout his life, and much of her popularity there traces back to him.

As a method

Read as a method, Tara is a movement the mind can learn: look straight at the danger and take the step before the fear has finished talking. The twenty-one forms of the classical praise work as a catalogue of how differently fear shows up, and of the response each kind asks for.
