Vajrapani

Vajrapani (Sanskrit: वज्रपाणि, Vajrapāṇi, “vajra in hand”; Tibetan: ཕྱག་ན་རྡོ་རྗེ, Chana Dorje) represents power in Buddhist philosophy: strength placed at the service of awakening.

Older than he looks

He is one of the oldest figures in Buddhist literature and art. In the Ambaṭṭha Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 3), a yakkha named Vajirapāṇi appears in the air above a young brahmin who refuses to answer the Buddha, holding a blazing iron thunderbolt. In the Gandharan sculpture of the first centuries CE he walks beside the Buddha as his bodyguard, often carved in the likeness of Heracles.

The wrathful form

In Tibet he appears dark blue, standing in a warrior's stance inside a mass of flames. The right hand raises the vajra, the left makes the threatening gesture; he wears a crown of skulls and a tiger skin. All of that ferocity is directed at obstacles to awakening, and never at people.

The three protectors

With Avalokiteshvara and Manjushri he forms the Rigsum Gönpo, the protectors of the three families: compassion, wisdom and power. Tibetans often paint or carve the three side by side, as a reminder that each one works badly alone. Power with no compassion turns cruel, and compassion with no power stays helpless.

As a method

Anger carries a lot of energy, and most of it gets wasted in swallowing or exploding. Read as a method, Vajrapani is the strength inside anger, taken back from fear and aimed at the right place.

Sources

  • Ambaṭṭha Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 3
  • Gandharan sculpture of the Kushan period, where Vajrapani accompanies the Buddha
  • Tibetan tradition of the Rigsum Gönpo, the protectors of the three families
