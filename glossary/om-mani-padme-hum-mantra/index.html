Om Mani Padme Hum

Om mani padme hum (Sanskrit: ॐ मणि पद्मे हूँ, oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ; Tibetan: ཨོཾ་མ་ཎི་པདྨེ་ཧཱུྃ) is the six-syllable mantra of Avalokiteshvara and the most widely recited mantra in Tibet. It is carved on mani stones, rolled by the thousand inside prayer wheels and painted on rock faces along mountain roads.

Where it comes from

Its earliest known source is the Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra, a Mahayana text usually dated to around the fourth or fifth century CE, which presents the six syllables as the heart of Avalokiteshvara.

“The jewel in the lotus”?

The popular translation is only one reading. Many scholars take maṇipadme as a single name in the vocative, addressing Avalokiteshvara as “the one of the jewel and lotus”. Both readings meet in the same pair. The 14th Dalai Lama explains the jewel as method, the altruistic intention to awaken, and the lotus as wisdom; the mantra points to the two joined.

Six syllables

Tibetan commentaries often link each syllable to one of the six perfections, or to one of the six realms of rebirth whose suffering it is said to close off. The mantra is short on purpose: it can be repeated while walking, working or waiting.

As a method

Recited with attention, it works as a point of return: a short sound that brings the mind back to compassion when it drifts toward grasping.

Sources

  • Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra
  • Donald S. Lopez Jr., Prisoners of Shangri-La (1998), chapter on the mantra
  • Tenzin Gyatso, 14th Dalai Lama, Kindness, Clarity, and Insight (1984)
